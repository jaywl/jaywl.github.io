About

Jay is an integrative biologist. He leverages deep experience in several fields to study the ecology and evolution of collective behavior. Currently a postdoc at the Department of Computational Biology at Cornell University, he has worked in evolutionary biology, behavioral ecology, systems neuroscience, and infectious disease epidemiology. He merges methods and concepts from these fields to try to understand the foundational rules of life. Check out his research.

Research

Jay Love's research program tries to understand how and why collective behaviors evolve.

Collective behaviors, like starling murmurations or fish schools, are some of the most beautiful and historically enigmatic patterns that exist in the living world. Despite decades of study from a mechanistic perspective, little is known about the ecological importance and evolutionary relevance of these phenomena.

Our research group uses cutting-edge computational methods and massive datasets to unlock new questions about the role of emergent collective phenotypes in the evolution and diversification of life. Critically, we work from a mechanistic understanding of behaviors at the individual level that lead to collective behavior, leveraging deep experience in physiology and neuroscience. Because many (in fact, most) behaviors exist within social context, our study of organismal social behavior is always done in the service of a greater understanding of how social systems came to be the way that they are, if and how the particular structure of a social system matters to ecology and evolution, and, overall, what role collective phenotypes have played in the evolution and diversification of life.

Collective Movement

Most research on collective behavior is focused on the way that local interactions between individuals in space produce emergent group-level patterns of movement. We call this phenomenon collective movement. It has long been thought that collective movement helps individuals to avoid predation and better exploit patchy resources, but testing these hypotheses has proven difficult.

We have published some of the first research to successfully put these hypotheses to the test at a broad scale. By first characterizing the evolutionary patterns that collective movement has undergone, we are able to look at evolutionary correlations between collective phenotype and other features of species' ecology.

In an upcoming paper, Jay and a smaller group of colleagues expand this type of analysis to the majority of the vertebrate clade. In the future, other collective behaviors in other taxonomic groups will be studied. By developing a clearer picture of how collective behaviors evolve through this comparative research, we hope to understand why collective behaviors evolve. This comparative work forms the groundwork from which more focused study into the ecological importance and genetic and neural underpinnings of collective behavior.

The reason that this work had not been done previously is because of the difficulty associated with quantifying the collective phenotype for many species. To overcome this barrier, my colleagues and I develop cutting-edge machine learning pipelines to process large text corpora and image databases, creating fully reproduceable phenotype data for thousands of species. I am very engaged in realizing the promise of these technologies for comparative biology and conservation ecology, and would be happy to collaborate on your project - get in touch here.

Communication

A trusted colleague once asked me why I was interested in studying sociality, because, in their words, "everything is social". They are right that all organisms interact with other organisms. However, once we start looking closely across many taxa, we quickly realize that there is abundant variation in the degree of sociality.

I have a long history of studying intraspecific social communication in animals. Working from a functional/mechanistic perspective, I try to understand the ecological setting and evolutionary importance of features of signal-receiver systems. Along with traditional observation and simple-but-effective low-impact experimentation in the field, I use creative computational tools to peer deeply into natural systems.

Some highlights include describing interspecific variation in the learned components of birdsong and finding a genetic basis for song rhythm (related to respiratory motor patterning) in the hermit-Townsend's warbler hybrid zone in the pacific northwest, where I have conducted field work for many years. I was also fortunate to contribute to collaborative work on the evolution of the developmental pathway for the avian syrinx.

I also spent a year establishing an experimental system to study the neural bases for mating behavior and ultrasonic vocalizations in lab mice. Using cutting-edge opto- and chemogenetic tools, we were able to manipulate and record neural activity of animals freely behaving in social settings. This research had a focus on understanding the impacts of social density stress, which relates directly to my work on the evolution of collective behavior. Jay and his mentor for this work wrote a short paper that discusses this, and the larger project continues today.

Contagion

In the first two papers conducting broad-scale phylogenetic comparative analyses of collective behavior, we have made the observation that, in all major clades that have been studied so far (fish, birds, and mammals), there are more evolutionary losses of collective behavior than there are gains. Why this occurs may have something to do with a downside of doing collective behavior: the increased potential for the spread of contagion.

Contagion with negative fitness consequences comes in at least two forms: misinformation and infectious disease.

Misinformation occurs naturally in systems where information is transmitted between individuals and is used to guide individual decisions. If the cost of misinformation outweighs the benefit of information spread, the tendency to form collectives may be selected against. I collaborate on research that places the spread of misinformation in a natural context.

Although collective behavior can confer fitness benefits to group members, acting in a collective increases an indiviual's probability of being infected with a transmissible pathogen, and so we expect an evolutionary trade-off based on these effects. To understand the evolutionary feedback between infectious disease and collective behaivor, we use mechanistic modeling and comparative genomics to characterize the fitness effects of infectious disease across different forms of collective behavior. We also interface with applied human infectious disease epidemiology, where our results have helped to guide public health policy.
